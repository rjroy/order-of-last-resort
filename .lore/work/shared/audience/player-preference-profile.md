# Player Preference Profile — Game Design Reference

**Purpose:** Help a game-design agent propose, critique, and refine games for this particular player. This is an evidence-based design brief, **not** a genre checklist or a mandate to combine every preference into one game.

**Source:** Extended conversation about games the player enjoyed, replayed, abandoned, and why. Statements below distinguish direct reports from design inferences.

## North star

> Give me something enjoyable to do, somewhere interesting to be, and something worth discovering.

The goal is **an experience the player is glad to have had**, not maximum playtime, retention, completion percentage, or replayability. A satisfying one-hour experience can succeed as fully as a thousand-hour one.

## Strongly supported preferences

### 1. Constructive systems with character

The player enjoys assembling components into a coherent strategy, seeing it become effective, and refining it **without destroying its identity**. Examples: Borderlands 2 character builds, Lorcana decks, factory/train networks in Satisfactory, rides in Planet Coaster. In Lorcana, the player says: **“I don't collect cards, I collect decks.”** They frequently lose but still enjoy the distinct experience each deck produces. A deck is often kept as-is; improvements are welcome when they preserve or enhance its character.

**Design implication:** Offer meaningful, legible combinations and multiple viable playstyles. Don't equate fun with maximum DPS, efficiency, or win rate. Avoid balance structures where experimentation means hours of ineffective play or a mandatory community build.

### 2. Mechanics that remain engaging while discovery unfolds

Enjoyable gameplay keeps the player present for authored discoveries. Tiny Tina's Wonderlands is a standout: strong Borderlands-style mechanical depth made the emotional framing story about a young GM processing grief land powerfully. In Satisfactory, factory and train construction was enjoyable, but the sparse story, tier unlocks, and collectible audio were essential motivation; the player estimates they would have played **roughly one-quarter as long** without the story.

**Design implication:** Make the activity rewarding now and give a credible reason to continue. A thin but well-paced narrative can matter enormously. Don't make story a substitute for engaging gameplay in a long-form game.

### 3. Authored personality, surprise, and recontextualization

Art, writing, quests, music, humor, atmosphere, and world identity matter. The player especially values revelations that change what the experience means. Tiny Tina's Wonderlands was their favorite in its series because the apparent fantasy-hero adventure concealed a more personal story. Story is often the best **delivery mechanism for personality**, not automatically the central attraction.

**Design implication:** Establish a recognizable voice and keep revealing new facets of it. Authored content can supply something that endlessly recombined procedural content cannot: an intentional perspective, surprise, or emotional payoff.

### 4. Meaningful feedback on player creations

In Planet Coaster, simulated visitors and ride grades made creating and refining story-driven dark rides and coasters more satisfying than construction alone. In Satisfactory, functioning planet-scale logistics made train travel enjoyable in its own right (the player sometimes rode a ~30-minute loop). In Lorcana, playing a deck tests the experience it was built to create.

**Design implication:** Let the world or game systems respond to the *specific character* of what the player made. Evaluation should enable iteration, not collapse every creation into one optimal answer.

### 5. Novelty through meaningful interactions, not superficial permutations

Borderlands 2 loot could invite rethinking a build. Wildermyth's character builds sustained many short campaigns, but after ~40 hours builds converged and emergent stories had diminishing returns. Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis reactions felt novel once, then quickly recognizable and repetitive. The player described it as a series of obvious conditionals rather than convincing emergence.

**Design implication:** Prefer interacting systems that yield genuinely different decisions and experiences. More variants, names, scars, dialogue branches, or procedural events do not by themselves create depth. Conditional rules *can* yield emergence; the issue is whether the resulting experience remains surprising and consequential.

### 6. Agency over activity and degree of engagement

Guild Wars 2 has supplied thousands of hours because the player can switch among combat, builds, story, exploration, group events, and cosmetic pursuits according to mood, while these activities still advance broader goals. This is a strength of the MMO's “theme park” structure, **not a requirement for every game**.

**Design implication:** In long-form designs, multiple activities can feed shared progression. Avoid punishing the player for choosing an interesting mode over the mathematically optimal one.

### 7. Shared spectacle with genuine stakes, without social obligation

In Guild Wars 2's large (~40+ player) events, spectacle makes participation worthwhile even in failure. The possibility of failure makes each contribution matter; success produces a feeling of **“look what WE did”**, even without speaking to anyone.

**Design implication:** If proposing cooperative play, consider visible collective consequences and low-friction participation. The player does not require chat, guild obligations, or direct socializing to enjoy belonging to a group achievement.

### 8. Bounded, restorative accomplishment

Logic Town's daily logic puzzles provide a ~30-minute chill/reset through **a small problem that can be completely resolved**. The player turns off leaderboards because comparison detracts from the experience. They will use hints on LinkedIn daily puzzles to finish. Childhood nostalgia may be a contributing factor, so do not extrapolate this into a blanket preference for all puzzles.

**Design implication:** Short-form games can succeed through satisfying closure rather than elaborate progression, competition, or endless difficulty escalation.

### 9. Different games can demand different emotional budgets

Florence was a worthwhile, under-two-hour, emotionally intense experience that told its story through interaction. The player would recommend it but does not want to replay it. Borderlands 2 offered ~80 hours of lighter immersion, mechanical engagement, and personality. The player explicitly contrasts long periods of **light immersion at low emotional cost** with short bursts of **heavy immersion at emotional cost**.

**Design implication:** Choose a coherent engagement mode. Do not assume emotional intensity should be sustained for dozens of hours, or that every game needs deep builds. A game can move between modes, but should respect their cost.

### 10. Gameplay should justify the medium

I Was a Teenage Exocolonist stood out as a visual novel combined with deckbuilding. The player replayed its two substantially different storylines but did not want to reread much of the same text for minor nuances. Deckbuilding is independently one of their core mechanics (Slay the Spire, Monster Train, Lorcana, etc.), but they do not play purely for endless mastery: eventually they ask, **“Okay got it, where's the story?”** They generally do not seek text-only visual novels: **“If I want to read a story I'll read a book.”** Florence shows that simple interaction can still justify a game when it meaningfully expresses the story.

**Design implication:** Integrate interaction and experience. Replays should offer substantive differences, and should not require excessive rereading of unchanged narrative.

## What makes them stop

| Failure mode | Evidence | Design warning |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanics become a chore relative to discovery | Book of Hours: compelling world, multiple attempts, eventually too much work to keep exploring | Reduce repeated friction between player and desired revelations |
| Personality runs out of new things to say | South Park: The Stick of Truth: humor carried a playthrough, but eventually the player wanted to finish; no desire for more | A single joke, tone, or premise cannot substitute for development |
| Apparent emergence reveals a shallow repertoire | Shadow of Mordor: Nemesis exciting once, predictable by second encounter | Don't market cosmetic reactive scripts as deep systemic narrative |
| Build variety converges and procedural stories plateau | Wildermyth after ~40 hours | Know when a game has delivered its value; authored content can complement systemic play |
| Massive complexity still forces a prescribed meta | Path of Exile 2: ~250 hours, but effective play increasingly meant following builds | Protect viable player authorship; complexity is not freedom |
| Familiar content must be repeated to reach tiny new variations | Exocolonist: two major storylines enjoyed; third reading unattractive | Offer meaningful differences or skip/condense familiar material |
| Repetition lacks sufficient forward development | Shadow of Mordor vs. an Assassin's Creed finished partly for its story | Repeating actions is acceptable; repeating the same experience without development is not |
| A build is interesting but too ineffective to enjoy playing | Borderlands 2: player expects to choose power over reinvention if the rules punish experimentation | Don't require self-handicapping to access novelty |

## Nuances and tensions to preserve

- **Victory vs. character:** Lorcana decks can lose and still be satisfying; an underpowered Borderlands build can be frustrating. Failure cost and moment-to-moment feel matter.
- **Challenge vs. relaxation:** Genuine failure makes GW2 collective events meaningful; leaderboards undermine restorative Logic Town puzzles. Challenge is contextual.
- **Creation vs. purpose:** Planet Coaster construction and Satisfactory logistics are fun, but meaningful grading, scenarios, story, and progression sustain them. Creation alone is not always enough.
- **Player authorship vs. designer authorship:** The player likes inventing strategies and experiences *and* encountering a designer's deliberate perspective. Neither replaces the other.
- **Long vs. short:** Satisfactory (~600 hours), Planet Coaster (~180), PoE2 (~250), GW2 (thousands), Wildermyth (~40), Logic Town (~60 in daily sessions), and Florence (<2) are all evidence of different successful experiences, not a preferred runtime.
- **Replaying vs. moving on:** Borderlands 2 characters were tried to see what they offered; several stopped ~1/3 through because their playstyles were not appealing. One character was completed twice, another once. Satisfactory was restarted once because rebuilding a flawed planet-scale factory was easier than fixing it, not because restarting was intrinsically appealing.
- **Observing vs. constructing:** Riding Satisfactory trains could be enjoyable for long stretches; observing Planet Coaster rides was enjoyable briefly; old Lorcana decks are occasionally revisited. There is no universal preference for building over watching.

## Design heuristics for future agents

Use these as questions, **not pass/fail requirements**:

1. What is intrinsically enjoyable about the player's next 5–15 minutes?
2. What can the player configure, combine, express, or meaningfully decide? (If little, what interactive experience justifies that?)
3. How does the game acknowledge the player's specific decisions or creations?
4. What new mechanic, authored moment, perspective, or revelation becomes available later?
5. What prevents repeated actions from becoming repeated *experiences*?
6. Can an interesting strategy remain viable without obeying a single meta?
7. Does the world have a distinct personality that develops rather than repeating one note?
8. What emotional and cognitive commitment does the game ask for, and for how long?
9. When has the experience delivered enough? Is a graceful ending better than more content?
10. What is the smallest playable prototype that could disprove the central appeal?

When pitching a concept, identify its **primary mode** (e.g., constructive systems, authored interactive narrative, restorative puzzle, cooperative spectacle) and at most a few supporting modes. Explain its core activity, feedback, purpose, authored payoff, likely repetition failure, and expected session/total length. Explicitly state which preferences the design intentionally **does not** serve.

## Evidence ledger — games discussed

- **Borderlands 2:** Favorite to experience again without memory; character builds, changing loot, distinctive personality; ~80 hours. Several classes sampled rather than completed because their playstyles were not appealing.
- **Tiny Tina's Wonderlands:** Favorite in its series; mechanical depth made the grief-related meta-story emotionally effective.
- **Satisfactory:** ~600 hours, one restart; planet-scale factories and trains, tier/story/audio rewards, enjoyable train rides; without story estimated ~1/4 the time.
- **Guild Wars 2:** Thousands of hours; classes, flexible activities, ongoing authored story, large shared events, cosmetics, collective accomplishment.
- **Wildermyth:** ~40 hours; repeated short campaigns and character builds; eventually converging builds and limited emergent storytelling.
- **I Was a Teenage Exocolonist:** Two main narrative paths replayed; deckbuilding + visual novel combination; repetition of familiar text limits replay.
- **Florence:** <2 hours, memorable mechanics-driven emotional storytelling; no replay desired.
- **Logic Town:** ~60 hours, daily bounded logic puzzles; restorative closure; leaderboards off.
- **Lorcana:** Deck creation as the collectible unit; strategy identity more important than win rate; iterative improvement without losing character.
- **Slay the Spire / Monster Train:** Familiar deckbuilding references; mechanical engagement matters, but mastery alone does not sustain interest indefinitely.
- **Book of Hours:** Compelling world/atmosphere despite increasingly chore-like mechanics; tried multiple runs.
- **South Park: The Stick of Truth:** Personality and humor sustained completion despite merely adequate mechanics; no replay or sequel interest.
- **Shadow of Mordor:** Promising franchise, skill trees, Nemesis; quickly predictable reactive storytelling, weak narrative motivation, unfinished.
- **Assassin's Creed (unspecified entry):** One game completed despite eventually repetitive stealth/combat because story sustained it; not evidence of liking all entries.
- **Path of Exile 2:** ~250 hours; vast interconnected systems, but pressure to follow effective community builds eventually reduced appeal.
- **Planet Coaster:** ~180 hours; authored rides, scenery influencing simulated guests, ratings, scenarios, experimenting with story-driven dark rides and coasters.

## Open questions / limits of evidence

- Which specific styles of combat, control feel, pacing, difficulty, and interface friction are preferred?
- How much mechanical opacity is welcome before discovery becomes work?
- What forms of narrative pacing, writing, and genre are preferred or disliked?
- Which production constraints matter for games built personally (solo scope, tools, art budget, platform)?
- Several original Steam links were not yet individually explored in the conversation; do not invent preferences from unidentified titles.

**Agent instruction:** Treat explicit player statements as evidence, the synthesis as revisable interpretation, and your own design ideas as hypotheses. Challenge this profile with concrete counterexamples rather than flattering the player or assuming one game should contain every appealing mechanic.
