# Game hooks and player promises

## Key Findings

Mind Garage needs a starting point before its six-variant mechanic exploration: a provisional account of who the game is for, what experience it promises, and why that experience might stand out. A hook helps articulate that direction; it does not establish enjoyment.

The useful distinction is not marketing versus design, but the question each test answers: attention, comprehension, enjoyment, or voluntary replay. Evidence for one does not automatically establish the others.

Do not require a polished USP before allowing prototypes. A hook can motivate a prototype, but a prototype can also reveal the hook. The proposed upstream process below is a synthesis for this project, not a validated universal method. No game concept or new skill is approved by this research.

## Terms for this project

These are working distinctions; industry usage overlaps.

| Term | Working meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Players whose existing preferences and play contexts make the proposed experience relevant |
| Player fantasy or experience promise | What the player hopes to do or feel, not necessarily a fictional character role |
| Hook | A concrete reason to notice or become curious about the game |
| USP | A claim about why this game is a distinctive, worthwhile choice relative to alternatives |
| Anchor | A familiar frame that helps players understand the offer |
| Core mechanic | The repeated action, decision, and consequences through which the player interacts |

A hook need not be a mechanic. It can involve presentation, narrative, social interaction, or an unusual experience. A distinctive combination can matter without inventing a genre or proving nobody has ever done anything similar.

## Source findings and limits

### Hooks and anchors: useful marketing vocabulary

Akupara Games explicitly describes its hooks-and-anchors exercise as primarily marketing. Its hook is the attention-grabbing novelty; its anchor is the familiar gameplay or genre frame. It illustrates gameplay with the Nemesis system in *Shadow of Mordor*, aesthetics with *Spinch*, and narrative with *Mutazione*.

The useful lesson is to communicate both the difference and the recognizable experience. Its assertions about what sells games and what players prefer are practitioner claims, not controlled causal findings. We need not adopt its strongest formulation that a hook must be something no game has ever done.

Source: [Akupara Games, Indie Insights: Hooks and Anchors](https://www.akuparagames.com/2020/12/09/indie-insights-hooks-and-anchors/).

### Concept testing: investigate experience rather than hypothetical purchases

Games User Research warns that asking whether someone would play or buy a concept is a poor predictor of future behavior. It recommends discussing actual prior play and purchases, showing material that conveys the intended experience, and probing what interests or concerns people and how those concerns relate to past behavior.

This is practitioner guidance from a source that also promotes research services and materials. Its method is more useful here than a promise of commercial success. Statements of interest can inspire hypotheses; they do not validate future purchases or enjoyment. Even past-behavior reports remain self-reports.

Source: [Games User Research, Avoid the traps of concept testing](https://gamesuserresearch.com/avoid-the-traps-of-concept-testing/).

### A development example: mechanics can precede the final theme

The developers of *Let’s! Revolution!* describe starting from “What if we applied the rules of Minesweeper to a path?” They tested it using a digital whiteboard before coding. Early prototypes were not yet fun; movement and abilities became an important breakthrough. The final narrative and visual direction was selected later through internal concept exploration around existing prototypes.

This demonstrates a possible route from a small mechanical question to an articulated player experience. It does not show that mechanics-first is always best, or that a catchy initial premise guarantees fun. It is a retrospective account by the developers, not a controlled comparison of methods.

Source: [Michael Highland and Ian Sundstrom, Prototyping Let’s! Revolution!](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/prototyping-i-let-s-revolution-i-transforming-i-minesweeper-i-into-a-turn-based-strategy-roguelike).

## Proposed upstream process

1. **Set the exploration envelope.** Identify intended players, platform/input assumptions, development constraints, and whether the experiment seeks design learning, commercial potential, or both. These can vary by experiment.
2. **Find an experience worth investigating.** Start from an interesting action, player desire, frustration with an existing game, fantasy, or surprising combination. Consider a few directions without requiring six complete game ideas.
3. **Write a provisional promise.** Explain the familiar frame, the difference, and why it might matter to those players. Plain language matters more than a polished slogan.
4. **Investigate adjacent games.** Look for similar experiences and how this direction differs. Record known comparisons and uncertainty rather than claiming exhaustive originality.
5. **Probe the largest uncertainty cheaply.** Use discussion of relevant past play for audience assumptions, representative concept material for comprehension or presentation questions, and a tiny playable interaction when the question depends on play. Do not generate assets or build prototypes without applicable authorization.
6. **Feed a bounded question into `explore-mechanic`.** Pass the audience, experience promise, familiar frame, constraints, candidate mechanic, and open hypotheses into its six-version flow. Revisit the promise when play reveals something better.

A useful provisional sentence is: “For these players, this offers this recognizable experience, but with this difference, which might let them do or feel this.” This is a reasoning aid, not a mandatory pitch formula.

## Keep the tests distinct

| Question | Suitable evidence | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the presentation attract attention? | Reactions or appropriately designed presentation comparisons | Attention can reflect spectacle or confusion; it is not enjoyment |
| Do players understand the promise? | Ask them to explain what they expect to do | A comprehensible promise can still produce a dull game |
| Is the interaction enjoyable? | Observed play and reports tied to a build and session | Small samples and rough implementation limit generalization |
| Do players voluntarily return? | Actual voluntary replay, separately from requested test runs | Replay does not by itself explain enjoyment or motivation |

Clicks, wishlists, and hypothetical interest are not substitutes for play evidence. A later commercial experiment may investigate acquisition, but that is not a prerequisite for a design-learning experiment. No universal conversion threshold is supported by this research.

## What is missing today

- A provisional audience and play context, rather than an abstract appeal to everyone.
- A desired player experience, not just a novel rule.
- Platform/input and scope constraints that make the first experiment feasible.
- A distinction between making something worth playing and making its value easy to communicate.
- A comparison with existing experiences, without turning novelty research into an impossible proof of originality.

The immediate recommendation is a short player-promise exploration before mechanic selection, with permission to loop back after prototyping. Do not change the existing six-alternative mechanic convention into six mandatory game pitches or a marketing-first gate.

## Research limits

The three sources above were directly fetched and checked on the research date. They provide terminology, practical methods, and a concrete development history. This is a selective practitioner-source synthesis, not a systematic review or empirical proof that a hook-first process improves games. Existing project notes remain the basis for separating mechanical diagnostics, player reports, enjoyment, and replay.
